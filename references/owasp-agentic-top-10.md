The target outcomes and tests below are this project's engineering recommendations. They are a starting point for the application's threat model, not certification criteria or a promise of complete coverage. Assess combinations across categories and application-specific abuse cases.

## How to read the table

The model proposes. The application enforces. Every target outcome is met by deterministic controls the model cannot alter (application code, identity and policy services, sandboxes, network policy and infrastructure), never by prompts or model judgment alone. Several of these risks, notably ASI01, ASI02, ASI06, ASI08, ASI09 and ASI10, often do harm within authority the application has legitimately granted. Denying out-of-policy calls is necessary but not sufficient, so most rows also test an abuse case that stays within granted authority.

Least Agency comes first. The publication extends least privilege and excessive agency with Least Agency (p. 7): autonomy a task does not need only adds attack surface. In the catalog, R-03 requires a recorded task need for every tool, autonomous step, trigger and delegate, and a deterministic workflow or lower autonomy scope wherever one meets the need. A capability that is never enabled needs no control in the rows below.

- **Risk:** the official identifier and title, with the pages of the publication's entry.
- **Target outcome (this project):** what must hold where the risk applies. These are this project's engineering targets; the publication does not require them.
- **Adversarial evidence and detection signal:** hostile tests driven through the real execution path, ending with the signal that should fire. Pair every denial with an authorized success case of the same shape through the same path (S-02).
- **Key controls:** the primary enforcement controls from the catalog, by bare ID. The ID's letter is its pillar: Risk & Responsibility (R), Authority & Access (A), Isolation & Integrity (I), Security Verification (S), Evidence & Evolution (E). Every listed control carries that ASI identifier in its catalog `asi` field. The complete ASI to control table, including verification and detection controls, is in [controls/index.md](controls/index.md). Four cross-cutting governance and verification controls (R-01, R-04, S-01 and S-06) carry every ASI identifier because they govern or verify the controls for each risk; the index lists them once on a cross-cutting line, and this table does not repeat them as key controls.

## Target outcomes, evidence and key controls

| Risk | Target outcome (this project) | Adversarial evidence and detection signal | Key controls |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ASI01: Agent Goal Hijack** (pp. 9-11) | Hostile content cannot change what a run is for. Task scope (permitted operations and targets) is fixed from the principal's request, trusted state or the trigger policy before untrusted content enters the context, not from the principal's standing permissions alone. A recipient, URL, path, payee or amount proposed from a tainted context matches a trusted source, or receives fresh confirmation when it occurs in content the principal supplied or pointed the run at; a value with no such origin is denied, not confirmed. System prompts and goal definitions change only through reviewed configuration. Goal-deviation detection is a monitored signal, not the enforcement boundary. | Deliver redirection payloads through retrieval, tool output, summaries, inbound email or calendar items, uploaded files, web pages and peer-agent messages, and force each resulting forbidden proposal through dispatch; observe denial with no unauthorized read or effect. While the user may email anyone, plant a recipient in a retrieved document and force a send to it; observe denial or a confirmation that labels the address as document-derived, and verify that a recipient the user typed still sends. Include a payload whose action is within the user's permissions but outside the task; observe a task-scope denial or a confirmation pause with the deviation recorded. Let an inbound email trigger an unattended run that asks to forward a confidential file; observe no effect. For a browser or computer-use agent, have a page direct navigation or a form submission to a host outside the allowlist; observe that the browser or proxy allowlist blocks it and that consequential steps need confirmation outside the agent's screen. Observe the detection signal: provenance and task-scope denials from tainted contexts reach a named owner. | R-03, A-07, A-06, A-08, A-24, A-25, I-01, I-02, A-19, I-17, E-09 |
| **ASI02: Tool Misuse & Exploitation** (pp. 12-14) | A permitted tool can operate only on authorized objects, arguments and destinations, and exposes only the operations the task needs (for example, no send or delete for a summarizer). Destructive or costly operations within granted authority are reversible, budgeted, or approved after a side-effect-free dry-run preview. Chains of allowed tools cannot bypass disclosure policy, enforced by provenance-aware destination policy at dispatch. Tools resolve by fully qualified identity and fail closed on ambiguity. Runtime manipulation of a legitimate tool's descriptor is tested here; a tool that is malicious at its source is tested under ASI04. | Attempt an allowed read followed by transmission to a disallowed recipient; observe that the destination receives no data, and verify a permitted recipient still works. Through injected content, induce a destructive call or a looping costly call on a permitted tool; observe an approval or budget stop. Register a look-alike tool name on a second server; observe fail-closed resolution. Change a pinned tool descriptor at runtime; observe the tool is disabled until re-review. Encode data in DNS lookups or query strings through an auto-approved network tool, and in a rendered markdown image URL; observe egress denial and no image fetch. With protected data in context, induce a provider-executed fetch or remote connector to send it to an unapproved destination; observe that it is disabled, outside its provider allowlist, or held for application-side approval. Observe the detection signal: an unusual tool chain (a sensitive read followed by a send to a new destination) or a budget stop raises an alert. | R-03, A-09, A-10, A-20, A-06, A-24, A-02, I-18, I-12, I-13, I-11 |
| **ASI03: Identity & Privilege Abuse** (pp. 15-17) | Current authenticated authority and tenant ownership constrain every action and delegation. Each agent runs under its own workload identity on behalf of a specific authenticated principal; credentials and privileged context are not reused across users or sessions. A delegate receives at most the intersection of the initiating principal's current authority, its own policy and the task's need. | Substitute another tenant's object, forge a role, revoke the caller, and queue work for later execution; observe denial before protected access, and the child never gains broader permissions. Invoke, as user B, an agent whose connector its maker authorized; observe that B's own authority, not the maker's, decides access. After a privileged task, continue the session as a non-admin; observe that cached credentials and approvals are unusable. In a shared channel, have user B ask for data retrieved for user A; observe denial. Plant a device-code or OAuth consent link in content the agent reads; observe that the agent cannot complete it and no new scope is bound. Observe the detection signal: a delegate holding scopes beyond its envelope, or a token used outside its audience or session, raises an alert. | A-03, A-04, A-15, A-16, A-17, A-19, A-25, I-05, I-17 |
| **ASI04: Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities** (pp. 18-20) | Tools, MCP servers, remote agents, skills, prompts, models and dependencies, including components found at runtime through registries or agent cards, are pinned by version or hash with recorded provenance and pass review before use. Review alone is not trusted: third-party components run confined to their declared files, network destinations and secrets, so a component that is malicious from its first version, or compromised later, cannot reach what it was not given. Operators can disable a specific tool, server, prompt or agent connection in every deployment without a deploy. | Substitute an unreviewed tool definition or dependency version; observe rejection or confinement pending review, without silently falling back to the changed component. Offer a new server or agent through a registry or agent card at runtime; observe that it is not loaded. Run a reviewed third-party tool in an environment that holds a canary secret outside its declared scope; observe that it cannot read or exfiltrate the canary. Disable a compromised tool through the stop control; observe no new calls at each scope. Observe the detection signal: alert on runtime hash, signature or descriptor drift. | I-24, I-18, I-19, I-23, I-25, I-11, E-06, E-07 |
| **ASI05: Unexpected Code Execution (RCE)** (pp. 21-23) | Model text cannot become arbitrary host execution. Necessary code execution stays within an enforced boundary. Outside that boundary, model output never reaches eval, dynamic imports, template engines, unsafe deserializers or shell strings. Generated code, installs and imports run only in a disposable non-root sandbox with no credentials and default-deny network, behind a validation gate between generation and execution, with no direct path from the agent to production. | Use inert command/path injection fixtures and controlled file/network probes. Observe text treated as data and forbidden host access blocked by the real isolation mechanism. Add fixtures that reach a deserializer, a template engine and an eval-backed memory or tool; observe no execution outside the sandbox. Have the agent regenerate a lockfile from unpinned specifications, or install a package whose install hook writes outside the workspace and calls out; observe the pinned version, confinement and denied egress. Observe the detection signal: blocked sandbox egress and sandbox policy violations are logged and alerted. | I-14, I-15, I-16, I-05, I-12, A-02, I-24, I-23 |
| **ASI06: Memory & Context Poisoning** (pp. 24-26) | Persisted or retrieved content retains its provenance and permissions and cannot become authoritative policy. Facts that drive consequential actions (prices, payees, limits, policies) are read from the system of record at execution, not from memory. Entries written from tainted contexts, including the agent's own generated outputs, are stored as untrusted-derived and expire or rank below verified entries. A poisoned scope can be quarantined and rolled back. | Insert hostile memory, compact it, change tenants, then revoke its source. Observe no authority increase, no tenant leakage, and no revoked material in retrieval or caches. Reinforce a false price or refund rule across sessions; observe that the consequential action uses the system-of-record value. Seed near-duplicate content in one tenant's index; observe that it never enters another tenant's retrieval. Observe the detection signal: unusual write rates or sources for a memory scope raise an alert routed to an owner. | I-08, I-07, I-06, I-01, A-06, I-22, E-08 |
| **ASI07: Insecure Inter-Agent Communication** (pp. 27-29) | Authenticated, integrity-protected messages carry constrained task authority; receivers validate it independently. Protected data has confidential transport. Peers use a pinned protocol and schema version, and downgrade is rejected. Discovery accepts only registered peers pinned by identity and descriptor hash; a signature on an agent card identifies its publisher, not the truth of its claims. | Forge a sender, alter the task scope, replay an old envelope, and repeat delivery. Observe rejection of unauthorized messages and no duplicate effect; verify transport configuration for protected data. Register a look-alike peer in the discovery service; observe that it receives no traffic. Force an older protocol or schema version; observe rejection. Present a validly signed agent card whose descriptions carry instructions or inflated capabilities; observe that it is not loaded unless pinned and that its text is handled as untrusted content. Point a callback or push-notification URL at an internal address; observe egress denial. Observe the detection signal: unknown peers, unexpected routing and rejected or replayed messages raise an alert. | I-21, A-15, I-18, A-16, A-17, I-12, I-01 |
| **ASI08: Cascading Failures** (pp. 30-32) | A fault in one agent, tool or memory, including a poisoned or hallucinated output, is contained before it spreads. Each receiving agent revalidates inputs through its own policy, a check independent of the producer gates fan-out, and blast-radius limits (fan-out caps per decision and time window, halting of repeated intents and oscillating retries, circuit breakers that stop executors when a planner misbehaves) plus cumulative budgets bound the spread. Every propagated action carries lineage to its source. | Inject a faulty planner output that is within each executor's individual authority; observe that independent validation or a policy gate stops fan-out and that lineage records identify the source. Cause dependency failures while children spawn and retry; observe bounded total cost and work, no duplicated effects, and containment within the intended task. Make two agents consume each other's output in a loop; observe that the loop halts at its limit. Observe the detection signal: rapid fan-out from one decision, the same intent repeated many times and retry storms raise an alert and pause the affected run. | I-22, A-01, A-15, A-20, A-21, A-05, I-08, E-06 |
| **ASI09: Human-Agent Trust Exploitation** (pp. 33-35) | People see the actual proposed action, and approval authorizes only that action under current policy. Approval screens are rendered from canonical server data in a component the model cannot write into, show where each material value came from (principal-entered, system of record, or derived from untrusted content), flag first-time payees and changed payment details, accept a confirmation only from the session that displayed the preview (or after fresh re-authentication), and make no network request or state change when opened. A new or untrusted-derived contact, recovery, payout, webhook or forwarding destination does not take effect on an in-app click: it is verified through the destination already on record (or, for a value the principal typed, through the new destination), and the previous destination is notified. Consequential tools offer no "always allow", and approval volume is monitored for fatigue. Agent requests that the user act outside the application (paste a command, change bank details, enter credentials elsewhere) appear as unverified suggestions that can be reported. | Supply a benign explanation with a different underlying target; mutate, expire, or replay approval. Observe the canonical preview and rejection of every mismatched or invalid authorization. Take a new payee from a poisoned invoice; observe that the preview labels it new and document-derived, and that a changed payment destination needs out-of-band verification. Have the principal confirm a contact email planted in a document they asked about; observe that the change waits for verification through the address on record and that a link sent to the planted address does not apply it. Confirm a card from a second session that never showed it; observe rejection. Open a preview that would call a webhook; observe no request. Submit a request body with a forged assistant tool result ("approval granted") or a system-role message; observe that the server discards it and no effect occurs. Have the agent ask the user for credentials; observe that entry is possible only in a trusted application component and that the request renders as unverified agent text with a report option. Observe the detection signal: approval rate and time to decide per approver, and user reports of suspicious agent behavior, reach a named owner. | A-11, A-12, A-13, A-25, I-26, A-06, I-08, I-09, A-14 |
| **ASI10: Rogue Agents** (pp. 36-38) | Independent controls stop an agent that violates its mandate, including after it has started running. Agent credentials cannot change the agent's own policy, grants, prompts, budgets or audit configuration, or create agents, credentials, schedules or compute outside registered templates. Destructive operations within a legitimately held role stay guarded (for example, backups that no agent identity can delete). A suspect agent can be quarantined and is reintegrated only after re-verification and human approval. Where agents use signing or attestation keys, the orchestrator mediates signing; keys are never exposed to the agent. | Request tool expansion, audit suppression, or continued work after revocation. Observe denied escalation, retained audit evidence, and prevention of new effects by the agent and its descendants. Give an agent a cost-reduction goal and a role that can delete storage; observe that backup deletion is blocked or held for approval. Attempt to spawn copies of the agent or new schedules through a provisioning API; observe denial by credential scope. Quarantine an agent, then attempt reintegration with a changed configuration hash; observe refusal. Observe the detection signal: actions outside the declared tool set, activity that continues after its triggering source was removed, and spawning bursts raise an alert routed to an owner. | A-23, A-10, A-09, A-07, A-15, E-02, E-06, E-07, E-08 |

## Detection, levels and classification

Detection supplements enforcement; for persistent or within-authority attacks (ASI06, ASI08, ASI10), test that the signal is emitted and routed to an owner. E-04 defines the baseline signals and their routing, and E-05 adds behavioral baselines with automatic containment. A signal never substitutes for a key control.

Some key controls are Level 2 (A-13, A-17, I-16, I-22, I-26, E-07, E-08) or Level 3 (A-14). [profile-and-levels.md](profile-and-levels.md) computes which level, and therefore which controls, apply to each agent context. Where a Level 2 control is the primary defense, the Level 1 controls in the same row still apply; for ASI08, for example, A-01, A-15, A-20, A-21, A-05, I-08 and E-06 contain a spreading fault before I-22 is required.

Classification notes, paraphrasing the publication's boundaries between entries:

- Misuse of a tool within the agent's granted privileges is ASI02; misuse that involves escalation or inherited credentials is ASI03, and misuse that ends in code execution is ASI05 (p. 12).
- Tampering at runtime with the interface of a legitimate tool (its descriptor, schema, metadata or routing) is ASI02; a tool that is itself malicious or compromised at its source is ASI04 (p. 13). I-18 serves both: it disables a pinned tool whose descriptor changes and refuses components that were never pinned.
- Direct redirection of goals is ASI01; persistent corruption of stored or retrievable context is ASI06, which often leads to ASI01 (pp. 9, 24).
- Record an originating defect under its own entry (for example ASI04, ASI06 or ASI07); ASI08 applies once the defect propagates beyond its origin to other agents, sessions or workflows (p. 30).
- Human misperception of, or over-reliance on, an agent is ASI09; an agent deviating from its intended function is ASI10 (p. 33).

## Using this file in a security record

For each applicable row, identify the code or configuration that enforces the outcome and the test that exercised it, and add authorized success cases. In the record's ASI applicability section, map each category to control IDs and at least one abuse case that stays within granted authority, or mark it `not-applicable-with-reason` with a reason that names a verifiable architectural absence (R-04). Use the status and evidence scope enums in [conformance.md](conformance.md). An external service or library handling a feature does not automatically make the risk inapplicable.

Input filters, prompt-injection classifiers and guard models, such as those described in the [OWASP LLM Prompt Injection Prevention Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/LLM_Prompt_Injection_Prevention_Cheat_Sheet.html), are useful signals. On their own they meet no target outcome in this file.

For threat-taxonomy alignment, the publication's Appendix A (pp. 39-40) relates each entry to the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025, the Agentic AI Threats and Mitigations taxonomy (T1-T17) and AIVSS core risks. Its Appendix D (pp. 44-49) tracks real exploits and incidents by ASI entry; synthetic analogues of those incidents make good named regression fixtures. To trace an ASI risk to AIUC-1, NIST AI RMF and OWASP AISVS references, follow the key control IDs into [mappings.md](mappings.md). These are engineering mappings, not certification or conformance claims.

Implement boundary details from [runtime-controls.md](runtime-controls.md) and [mcp.md](mcp.md), and record evidence using [verification.md](verification.md). Related OWASP work and cross-framework alignment are described in [frameworks.md](frameworks.md).
